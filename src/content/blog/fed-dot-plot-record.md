In six of the last ten years, the Fed’s December dot plot landed within a quarter point of where the Fed’s rate really ended the next year. In the other four it was off by 0.75 to 3.5 points. The dot plot is a chart on which each Fed official puts a dot where they think the rate should be at the end of each coming year. This post scores the median dot.

A quarter point is the size of a normal Fed change, so this post counts everything in quarter points. The next dot plot comes out on 9 December.

## 1. What one dot is

The Fed’s rate is the federal funds rate, what banks charge each other overnight. The Fed sets it as a range a quarter point wide, such as 3.75 to 4%. Four times a year it also publishes the dot plot, with one dot per official for each coming year.

The Fed’s note to the table says what a dot measures:

> “Each participant’s projections are based on his or her assessment of appropriate monetary policy.”

So each dot is where that official thinks the rate should be, given the economy they expect. The Fed prints the median dot, the middle answer, and that is the number the news reports.

Each median points at a range. A median of 1.4% is the middle of a 1.25 to 1.5% range, rounded. Every December median since 2015 lands on one of these middles. Turn the median back into its range and you can count it in quarter points from where the rate stood that December.

## 2. Ten Decembers, counted in quarter points

Take December 2021. The rate sat at 0 to 0.25%. The median dot for the end of 2022 was 0.9%, which is a range of 0.75 to 1%. That is three quarter points up. The Fed raised seven times in 2022, by 4.25 points in all, and ended the year at 4.25 to 4.5%. That is 17 quarter points. The dot was 14 quarter points short.

Here is every year the same way. Each row uses the dot set the December before that year, and every figure is in quarter points. A plus is up, a minus is down.

| Year | Dot planned | Fed did | Apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2016 | +4 | +1 | 3 |
| 2017 | +3 | +3 | 0 |
| 2018 | +3 | +4 | 1 |
| 2019 | +2 | −3 | 5 |
| 2020 | 0 | −6 | 6 |
| 2021 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 2022 | +3 | +17 | 14 |
| 2023 | +3 | +4 | 1 |
| 2024 | −3 | −4 | 1 |
| 2025 | −2 | −3 | 1 |

Two years were exact. Four were one quarter point apart. In each of those four, the Fed went one quarter point further than the dot, in the same direction. For 2023 the dot planned 0.75 points of increases and the Fed made 1 point. For 2025 it planned 0.5 points of cuts and the Fed made 0.75.

## 3. The four big misses

**Cuts the dot did not show.** The December 2018 dot planned two more increases for 2019. The Fed cut three times in the second half of 2019 instead. The December 2019 dot planned no change for 2020. The Fed cut 1.5 points in two steps in March 2020.

**Right direction, wrong size.** The December 2015 dot planned four quarter-point increases for 2016. The Fed made one, in December. The December 2021 dot planned 0.75 points for 2022, and the Fed raised 4.25.

The bond market prices its own path, and the 2-year Treasury yield is the easiest place to read it. How far the 2-year sat above the Fed’s September projections in late October is worked out in [the post on the Fed’s October decision](/blog/fed-decision-two-year-above-forecast). Why the 2-year paid more than the 10-year for most of 2022 to 2024, a stretch that began with the largest miss in the table, is in [the post on the yield curve’s recession signal](/blog/yield-curve-recession-signal).

## 4. This year’s dot so far

The December 2025 median for the end of 2026 was 3.4%. That is a range of 3.25 to 3.5%, one cut from the 3.5 to 3.75% range of that day.

The Fed raised instead. On 16 September it moved the range up to 3.75 to 4%. That already puts 2026 two quarter points from its dot, in the opposite direction.

September’s dot plot moved the 2026 median to 4.1%, the middle of 4 to 4.25%. That is one more increase before the year ends. If the Fed makes that increase on 9 December, the gap for 2026 is three quarter points.

We expect the new median for the end of 2027 to come in above 4.1%. Between June and September that median already rose from 3.6% to 4.1%. When it prints, count it in quarter points from the range the Fed sets that day. Over the last ten years the Fed then did that much, or one quarter point more, six times, and missed by three or more the other four.
