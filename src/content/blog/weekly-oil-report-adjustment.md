Oil traders watch one number every Wednesday: how much crude US companies added to their tanks or took out. The same report carries a line called the adjustment, for oil its own figures cannot place. From January 2024 to 6 November 2026, the adjustment was the bigger of the two in 41% of weeks. This year the share has fallen, to 27% so far.

## 1. The sum the report has to make

The report is the Weekly Petroleum Status Report from the U.S. Energy Information Administration, or EIA. It comes out at 10:30 a.m. New York time on Wednesdays, and on Thursday at noon after a Monday holiday. Its first table is a balance sheet for crude oil.

The balance works like a bathtub. Oil comes in from US wells, from imports and from some blending of other liquids. When the tanks are drawn down, that oil counts as coming in too. Oil goes out as exports and as crude run by refineries, and into the tanks when they fill. The two sides should match.

The storage figure is the one in the headlines. "US crude stocks fell 0.9 million barrels" is that line. It counts commercial stocks, the oil that companies hold in tanks and pipelines. It leaves out the government’s Strategic Petroleum Reserve.

## 2. The line that closes the gap

The two sides never land on the same number. So the table carries one more line, called "Adjustment". Its footnote is short.

> "Formerly known as Unaccounted-for Crude Oil, this is a balancing item."

The lines are not measured the same way. Stocks, imports and refinery runs come from a weekly survey of a sample of companies, the largest first. Stocks are a count of what sits in the tanks at the end of the week. EIA estimates domestic production from short-term forecasts, and exports come from customs records. An EIA article from June 2015 says the adjustment reflects "the combined uncertainty around each of the crude oil data elements".

## 3. One week, read line by line

The picture at the top is Table 1 for the week ending 30 October 2026, regrouped as oil in and oil out. As of 6 November it was the most recent week in which the adjustment was the bigger number. The figures are thousands of barrels a day, as the report prints them.

| Thousand barrels a day | Week ending 30 Oct 2026 |
|---|---|
| Oil in, tank draws included | 21,104 |
| Oil out | 20,400 |
| In minus out | 704 |
| Adjustment line | −704 |

Over seven days, 704 thousand barrels a day comes to about 4.9 million barrels. Commercial stocks fell 865,000 barrels that week, or 124 thousand barrels a day. The oil the report could not place came to more than five times the change in company tanks. The minus sign means the figures had more oil coming in than going out, so EIA takes 704 off to make the two sides meet.

## 4. How often the adjustment is the bigger number

The adjustment is printed per day, so we multiply it by seven. Then we compare its size with that week’s change in commercial stocks, whichever way each one points. EIA changed how the line treats those other liquids in November 2023. Before the change the line averaged about half a million barrels a day more on the plus side, so the counts start in January 2024. They run to the week ending 6 November 2026.

| Weeks | Count | Line bigger | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Since Jan 2024 | 149 | 61 | 41% |
| 2024 | 52 | 24 | 46% |
| 2025 | 52 | 25 | 48% |
| 2026, to 6 Nov | 45 | 12 | 27% |
| Last 52 weeks | 52 | 16 | 31% |

In the last 52 weeks the median size of the adjustment came to 2.2 million barrels over the week. The median size of the storage change was 3.4 million.

The largest adjustment of those 52 weeks came in the week ending 13 February 2026. It was minus 9.8 million barrels over the week, while commercial stocks fell 9.0 million.

## 5. What it changes on a Wednesday

A move in storage usually comes with a reason attached, such as "exports jumped" or "production slipped". EIA estimates production from a forecast. In a week when the adjustment is the bigger number, such a reason is a guess the table does not back. Table 1 shows which weeks those are, on the same page as the storage figure.
